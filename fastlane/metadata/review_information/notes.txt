WHAT CHANGED IN THIS VERSION

Queasy 1.0 was rejected twice under 1.1.6, for copy saying calming vibration
pulses relieve nausea in pregnancy. We took the point: a disclaimer cannot fix
a feature that claims to do something it cannot be shown to do, so this version
changes the product, not the wording. Queasy is now four separate things the
user does, each described by what it literally is and each showing its own
source in the app.

1. PULSE. The watch taps the inside of the wrist on a regular beat, 10 levels,
adjustable mid-session. This is the feature once described as acupressure. It
no longer is. The app states plainly, in Learn and in the description, that a
watch tapping your wrist is NOT acupressure and NOT nerve stimulation, that
those are different things done by bands that apply pressure and by cleared
devices that deliver current, and that no trial has tested a watch buzz in
their place. It is offered only as a regular sensation to focus on, and is the
one mode with no citation, which the app says outright.

2. BREATHE. A paced-breathing timer delivered as haptics: a swell on the in-
breath and a longer fall on the out-breath, so the user can pace their
breathing with their eyes shut. Source in app: trials of slow diaphragmatic
breathing on chemotherapy-induced and postoperative nausea, described in app as
small.

3. TONE. A generated 100 Hz sine tone for one minute at low volume. Source in
app with a link: Gu, Ohgami, Kagawa et al., "Just 1-min exposure to a pure tone
at 100 Hz with daily exposable sound pressure levels may improve motion
sickness", Environmental Health and Preventive Medicine, 2025-03-25, DOI
10.1265/ehpm.24-00247. The app quotes the paper's own "may improve" hedge
rather than restating it as a result.

4. PRESS. Instruction plus a timer. It shows how to locate the point on the
inner forearm that acupressure wristbands sit on, then times a three-minute
hold while the user presses with their own thumb or positions a band they
already own. The app does not claim to apply pressure, because it cannot.
Source in app with a link: the Cochrane review of interventions for nausea and
vomiting in early pregnancy, quoted as calling this evidence limited and
inconsistent.

WHAT THE APP SAYS IT IS NOT

Onboarding, every mode screen, Learn, Settings and the description state that
Queasy is a set of comfort techniques the user runs themselves, is not a
medical device, holds no clearance, does not diagnose, treat, cure or prevent
anything, and may not change how the user feels. They also state that
acupressure bands and wrist stimulation devices sold for nausea are cleared
medical devices, and Queasy is neither and no substitute for one. The Regulated
Medical Device declaration on this app is No and this version matches it.

MORNING SICKNESS is one occasion in the check-in alongside travel, hangover,
vertigo and a nervous stomach; the description lists it the same way. No
outcome is promised for it or any other occasion. Choosing it caps the pulse
intensity lower, lengthens the session and shows self-care guidance from NHS
and ACOG patient information, with a prominent note that sickness which stops
someone keeping fluids down needs a midwife or doctor, not an app.

All four modes are free and unlimited, with no account and no trial, so nothing
needs to be bought to review the app.

HARDWARE NOTE FOR TESTING

Two earlier reviews were performed on an iPad Air. Queasy is an iPhone-only
binary (TARGETED_DEVICE_FAMILY = 1). On iPad it runs in iPhone compatibility
mode, with no Core Haptics engine and no paired Apple Watch, so nothing can be
felt at all. Please use an iPhone, where Pulse and Breathe play through Core
Haptics, Tone through the speaker or headphones and Press runs as a timer; or
an iPhone with a paired Apple Watch, where the same patterns play on the wrist
and keep running with the screen off (WKExtendedRuntimeSession, physical-
therapy background mode, no HealthKit).
